# Intake - settle the brief before you build

You cannot design your way out of not knowing what the slide says. Nearly every thin slide traces back to a thin
brief: the agent did not know the message, did not know how much evidence existed, guessed the density, and
produced a title with three bullets because that is all the brief contained. Settle the five items below before
authoring HTML. Asking costs one message; building the wrong slide costs a round trip.

## The five

1. **The message.** One declarative sentence per slide: what the reader should believe afterwards. It becomes
   the title. If you cannot write it, you have a topic, not a slide.
2. **Audience and use.** Investor, client or internal; read cold or presented; part of a pack or standalone.
   Sets tone, what can be assumed, and whether sources are mandatory.
3. **Density tier - ask the user, do not assume.** Unless the brief already names a tier, ask in one line and
   explain the scale, for example: "How dense should these be? T1 = one idea given room (a cover, a hero
   number, three to five elements). T2 = a normal content slide: one primary visual done properly with labels,
   a synthesised read and a source (six to eight elements). T3 = investor or diligence grade: the visual plus
   a commentary rail plus supporting exhibits, caveats and source (ten or more). In between is fine, say
   'T2 leaning dense'." Record the answer per deck, or per slide when it differs. If the deck sits beside an
   external pack (a due-diligence report, an IM, bank material), read that pack first; it sets the bar and
   is the recommendation you attach to the question. Full definitions: `STYLE.md` section 6.
4. **The evidence.** The actual source for every number, quote and claim: file, tab, page. A slide is exactly as
   deep as its evidence; with one data point, say so rather than pad.
5. **The constraints.** What is off-limits or sensitive: internal-only material, unnamed clients, figures not yet
   cleared, files you must not overwrite. Ask explicitly before reusing internal material for an external audience.

## Ask, or assume and flag?

| Situation | Do this |
|---|---|
| The answer changes the slide's structure or content and you cannot determine it | Ask, batched into one message, with your recommended answer attached |
| You would have to invent a number, date, client, sequence or result | Ask. Never fabricate. If it must ship, mark `[ILLUSTRATIVE]` on the slide |
| Routine judgment a careful colleague would make (spacing, which grey, column order) | Decide it |
| Knowable from a file you can read | Read it |
| Several readings are plausible and all produce acceptable work | Pick one, state the assumption, keep building |

Ask in plain prose, two to four sharp questions, then keep building everything that does not depend on the
answers. Do not ask permission to begin, and do not ask "does this look right?" after building when the input
was knowable up front.

## Example

> Brief: "a slide on the Q3 results for the management team". Settled from the files: the message ("Q3 revenue
> beat plan by 6% on two large renewals, while margin slipped a point on hiring ahead of Q4"), audience
> (internal, presented), tier T2, evidence (the Q3 management accounts, tab "Summary"). Asked, batched: (1) the
> plan figure in the file is the May reforecast, not the budget - which comparison do you want? (2) the two
> renewals are named in the file - may they be named on the slide? Meanwhile built the layout and every element
> that did not depend on those two answers.

## After intake

For a new deck, ask in the same batched message which template to use and where to keep it (`SKILL.md` ->
Which template, Where the deck lives). Apply the template choice before designing: the palette you design
against comes from it.

The output of intake is HTML mockups the user reviews in the workroom, not a .pptx. See `PIPELINE.md`.
